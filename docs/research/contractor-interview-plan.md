# Contractor Interview Plan

**Status:** Ready for review

**Last updated:** 2026-09-13

## Research objective

Determine whether small residential general contractors and remodelers experience
a frequent, costly, and insufficiently solved gap between:

> **Documenting completed work → Receiving a homeowner decision → Preparing a
> milestone invoice**

The research should establish whether this segment is a credible launch customer
for StagePaid and identify how its real workflow differs from the current product
hypothesis.

## Decisions this research should inform

1. Should residential general contractors and remodelers remain the launch
   segment?
2. Which person inside the business owns the workflow and buying decision?
3. Which project types produce the strongest need?
4. What currently counts as stage completion and client approval?
5. How are corrections distinguished from additional scope?
6. Which evidence is already captured, and what does the homeowner actually see?
7. Which system currently creates the invoice?
8. Should StagePaid create invoices or hand approved billing data to an existing
   system?
9. What measurable outcome would make a provider adopt and pay for StagePaid?

## Research method

Conduct individual semi-structured interviews based on a specific recent
project. Interviews should last approximately 30–45 minutes.

Ask about past behavior before describing StagePaid. A product concept may be
introduced near the end to test comprehension, workflow fit, and willingness to
take a concrete next step.

This is directional product discovery, not a statistically representative market
study.

When a qualified participant cannot schedule a live interview, offer the
participant-facing [Contractor Workflow
Questionnaire](contractor-async-questionnaire.md) and request a short follow-up
conversation. Keep asynchronous responses distinguishable from live interviews
during synthesis. Do not send participants the internal interview note template
or evidence scorecard.

An asynchronous response may count as usable directional evidence when it:

- meets the participant criteria;
- discusses one specific recent project;
- provides sufficiently detailed behavioral examples; and
- can be responsibly sanitized and scored without filling gaps through
  researcher assumptions.

Mark its method as **Asynchronous questionnaire** in the research note. Treat
unsupported or ambiguous answers as lower-confidence evidence and attempt a
follow-up before counting them toward interview-specific thresholds.

## Sample

### Target

- Recruit 12 qualified participants.
- Complete at least 10 usable interviews.
- Include a mix of owner-operators and businesses with administrative or project
  support.
- Include more than one residential remodeling project type.
- Include a mix of current workflows, from mostly manual to software-assisted.

### Required participant criteria

A participant must:

- own, manage, or coordinate work for a residential general contracting or
  remodeling business;
- have direct knowledge of a multi-stage homeowner project completed or actively
  billed within the previous 12 months;
- participate in at least two of these activities: progress documentation,
  homeowner communication, approval, invoicing, or payment follow-up; and
- use milestone, progress, phase, or completion-based billing at least some of
  the time.

### Diversity goals

Across the sample, seek variation in:

- business size and administrative support;
- years in operation;
- project duration and type;
- average project complexity;
- geographic market;
- current invoicing and field-documentation tools; and
- comfort with client-facing software.

Do not recruit only technically enthusiastic providers. Adoption friction is
part of the research question.

### Exclude from this round

- people unable to discuss a specific recent project;
- contractors doing only single-visit service calls;
- providers paid entirely at the point of service;
- people working only on large commercial or public projects;
- vendors selling software or consulting services to contractors; and
- participants whose billing process is entirely controlled by another company's
  enterprise system.

## Recruitment screener

Ask these questions before scheduling:

1. What kind of residential work does your business perform?
2. What is your role in the business?
3. Have you completed or billed a project with multiple phases in the last 12
   months?
4. How was that project billed: upfront, by milestone, periodically, at
   completion, or another way?
5. Which parts of the process do you personally handle: documentation, homeowner
   communication, approval, invoicing, or payment follow-up?
6. Would you be comfortable discussing one recent project without sharing the
   homeowner's identifying information?

The screener should collect only the information needed to establish fit and
schedule the conversation.

## Recruitment message

> I’m researching how residential contractors document project stages, confirm
> completion with homeowners, and move approved work into billing. I’m looking
> for contractors or project coordinators willing to discuss one recent project
> in a 30–45 minute conversation. This is research, not a sales call, and you
> won’t need to share client names or confidential documents. Would you be open
> to participating or referring someone who fits?

Personalize the introduction and explain how the recipient was identified. Do
not imply an affiliation, endorsement, or prior relationship that does not exist.

## Before each interview

- Assign a participant identifier such as `C01`; do not use a name in committed
  research notes.
- Confirm that the participant meets the screener criteria.
- Explain the purpose, expected duration, and how notes will be used.
- Ask permission before recording audio or video.
- State that client names, addresses, financial account information, and
  confidential documents are not needed.
- Prepare a fresh copy of the interview note template.

## Opening script

> Thank you for speaking with me. I’m studying how residential contractors move
> from completed work to homeowner confirmation and billing. I’m interested in
> how you actually handle this today; there are no right answers, and I’m not
> evaluating you or your business.
>
> Please avoid sharing homeowner names, exact addresses, account information, or
> anything contractually confidential. I’ll use an anonymous participant code in
> the research notes.
>
> Is it okay for me to take notes? [If applicable: Is it okay to record this
> conversation for research review?]

If the participant does not consent to recording, continue with notes only. If
they do not consent to notes, end the research interview politely.

## Interview guide

Use the guide flexibly. Follow concrete events and unexpected details rather than
rushing to complete every question.

### 1. Business context

1. Tell me about your business and your role in it.
2. What kinds of residential projects make up most of your work?
3. Who normally communicates progress to the homeowner?
4. Who decides when a stage is billable and creates the invoice?

### 2. Recent-project walkthrough

> Think about the most recent project you billed in more than one stage. Please
> walk me through it from the original agreement to the latest payment.

Follow up with:

1. What were the stages or payment milestones?
2. Where were those stages documented?
3. How did everyone know what counted as complete?
4. Did the stages or expectations change after work began?

Keep the participant anchored to this project. Ask “What happened next?” when
answers become hypothetical.

### 3. Evidence and progress communication

1. What did you capture while the work was underway?
2. Where did photos, notes, checklists, or documents live?
3. Which of that information did the homeowner see, and how did they receive it?
4. Was any completed work later covered, enclosed, removed, or difficult to
   inspect?
5. Tell me about the last time you had to find old evidence or reconstruct what
   happened.
6. Which record would you trust if the homeowner remembered things differently?

### 4. Client review and approval

1. How did the homeowner know a stage was ready for review?
2. What did “approval” look like in practice?
3. Was approval ever assumed rather than explicitly recorded?
4. How long did the decision take?
5. What follow-up was required?
6. Tell me about a time approval was unclear or delayed.
7. What happened to the schedule or cash flow while you waited?

### 5. Changes and scope

1. Tell me about the last time a homeowner asked for something to change.
2. How was the request communicated and recorded?
3. How did you determine whether it corrected agreed work or added new work?
4. Who decided whether price or schedule changed?
5. How was the updated agreement documented?
6. Did the change affect an invoice or payment milestone?
7. What, if anything, was difficult about that process?

Use the participant's terminology. Do not introduce “Change Request” until their
existing language is understood.

### 6. Invoicing and payment handoff

1. What triggered creation of the milestone invoice?
2. Who created it, in which tool, and using what information?
3. Did anyone have to copy information from messages, photos, or another system?
4. How much time passed between completion, approval, and invoice issuance?
5. What commonly delayed payment after invoicing?
6. Could the homeowner understand what completed work the invoice represented?
7. What happened when invoice details differed from the homeowner's expectation?

### 7. Current tools and workarounds

1. Which tools were involved from project agreement through payment?
2. What purpose did each tool serve?
3. Where did information have to be entered more than once?
4. Which tool was the source of truth, if any?
5. What have you already tried to improve the process?
6. What works well enough that you would not want to replace it?
7. What does the current process cost in time, delayed cash, or client trust?

### 8. Concept reaction

Only after the behavioral interview, describe the concept neutrally:

> StagePaid is exploring a focused workflow where a contractor defines a payable
> project stage, attaches evidence when it is complete, and sends it to the
> homeowner for approval or a documented Change Request. Approval prepares a
> draft invoice for the contractor to review and issue.

Then ask:

1. What part, if any, fits the project you described?
2. What would not fit?
3. At which point would you stop using it and return to your current process?
4. What would the homeowner find confusing or burdensome?
5. Which existing tool would StagePaid need to work with?
6. What outcome would make this worth changing your process for?
7. Who besides you would need to agree to a pilot?

Avoid defending the concept. Confusion and objections are research findings.

### 9. Concrete next step

Interest alone is weak evidence. Ask for an appropriate commitment:

1. Would you review a prototype based on the project we discussed?
2. Would you introduce us to the person who handles billing or homeowner
   communication?
3. If the prototype addresses your concerns, would you consider testing it on a
   real upcoming project?
4. May we contact you for the next research round?

Record the exact commitment and any conditions. Do not describe a polite “maybe”
as pilot willingness.

## Closing script

> Thank you. Is there anything important about documenting stages, receiving
> homeowner approval, handling requested changes, or billing that I failed to
> ask? Is there another contractor or coordinator whose process differs from
> yours and who might be willing to speak with me?

Confirm any promised follow-up and do not subscribe the participant to marketing
communications without separate consent.

## Evidence scorecard

After each interview, score these dimensions using only behavior and evidence
from the specific project discussed:

| Dimension | 0 | 1 | 2 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Workflow fragmentation | One coherent system | Minor manual handoff | Several tools or reconstruction |
| Approval friction | No meaningful friction | Occasional ambiguity or follow-up | Repeated delay, dispute, or consequence |
| Evidence behavior | Little evidence needed | Evidence captured inconsistently | Evidence already captured and valued |
| Change/scope friction | Rare and clear | Some manual clarification | Recurring confusion or commercial impact |
| Billing handoff | Immediate and connected | Some re-entry or delay | Material rework or approval-to-invoice gap |
| Adoption fit | Workflow would not fit | Conditional interest | Clear fit and concrete next step |

The score organizes evidence; it does not replace interview notes or judgment.
Do not alter scoring definitions after seeing results without recording why.

## Predefined decision thresholds

Evaluate after at least 10 qualified, usable interviews.

### Continue with the segment

The initial hypothesis is supported when:

- at least 7 participants describe a multi-tool or manual handoff among evidence,
  approval, and invoicing;
- at least 6 provide a specific recent example of approval ambiguity, repeated
  follow-up, billing delay, or related commercial consequence;
- at least 6 already capture evidence they consider useful to the homeowner or
  project record;
- at least 5 describe the proposed stage-review workflow as compatible with
  their real project, without requiring a full construction suite first; and
- at least 3 make a concrete commitment to review a prototype or explore a
  real-project pilot.

### Modify the segment or workflow

Revise the hypothesis when the core pain is present but clusters around a
narrower project type, business role, billing method, or existing integration.
Examples include the problem appearing primarily in remodeling projects above a
certain complexity or only where an office coordinator manages billing.

### Stop or reconsider

Reconsider the segment when:

- fewer than 4 participants provide a recent example of meaningful
  approval-to-invoice friction;
- most participants consider informal approval sufficient;
- homeowner participation is consistently unacceptable;
- useful adoption requires specialized construction administration outside the
  MVP; or
- no qualified participant makes a concrete prototype or pilot commitment.

Thresholds are decision aids, not statistical claims. Contradictory evidence and
high-severity outliers should be discussed explicitly rather than hidden by a
count.

## Synthesis process

1. Complete and sanitize each interview note within 24 hours.
2. Separate direct evidence from researcher interpretation.
3. Update the scorecard only from the recorded interview.
4. Review patterns after interviews 5 and 10 without changing the final threshold
   retroactively.
5. Group findings by role, project type, billing method, and existing tools.
6. Identify evidence supporting and contradicting each launch-customer
   assumption.
7. Decide to continue, narrow, broaden, or reject the segment.
8. Update the launch-customer and product-brief documents with the result.
9. Record new product ideas in the backlog rather than silently adding them to
   the MVP.

## Research data handling

- Keep recruiting contacts and consent records outside the product repository.
- Use participant identifiers in repository documents.
- Store recordings only when explicit consent was given.
- Restrict raw research access to people who need it.
- Remove client and job-site identifiers during note preparation.
- Establish a retention period before collecting recordings or raw documents.
- Obtain separate permission before using an attributable quotation publicly.
- Treat deletion requests according to the research privacy process.
- Ask asynchronous participants to return responses through a private channel;
  do not collect completed questionnaires through the public repository.

## Deliverables

Issue 2 is ready for a segment decision when the repository contains:

- this reviewed interview plan;
- sanitized notes for each usable interview;
- a completed evidence scorecard;
- a synthesis of supporting and disconfirming patterns;
- a recorded launch-segment decision; and
- corresponding updates to the launch-customer and product-brief documents.
